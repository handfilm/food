Process

How we make it.

We won't publish the recipes. We will show you the system — the same discipline runs under all three products.

  1. 01

    Small batch

    Fixed batch sizes, planned before service, never scaled up mid-day.

  2. 02

    Controlled preparation

    Ingredients pre-portioned to weight and volume, not eyeballed.

  3. 03

    Fixed portions

    Every unit leaves the kitchen at the same size, every time.

  4. 04

    Fresh assembly

    Components are combined last, close to dispatch — not hours ahead.

  5. 05

    Delivery

    Packed to hold texture and temperature until it reaches you.

Limited daily production

We don't manufacture more than we can control.

Batch sizes are fixed on purpose. When a day's batch is gone, it's gone — not as a marketing device, but because consistency only holds within a batch size we can actually supervise by hand.

The Food of Bengal

Before it became a menu, it was a language.

Bangladesh has thousands of ways to cook what the world simply calls fish.

In Bangladesh, fish curry is not one dish. It is a system of relationships — between the fish, its size, the vegetable, how that vegetable is cut, the masala, the gravy, and knowing when not to add more. Every region, family, and kitchen can carry a slightly different answer.

This knowledge usually passes from person to person, not as a written, standardized recipe. Some of it is slowly fading from everyday use. Hands & Head wants to document and explore it — not as nostalgia, but as a system worth understanding.

Why fish?

A grammar hidden behind a generic label.

Commercial menus often compress an entire culinary system into a few words — fish curry, hilsa, prawn curry. But species, size, regional tradition, vegetable pairing, cut, masala intensity, gravy density, and cooking sequence can completely change what a dish actually is. That underlying structure is what we want to make visible, one preparation at a time.

Photography placeholder Shorshe Ilish
Ilish · সরিষা ইলিশ

Not one recipe. A family of recipes.

Ilish holds an exceptional place in Bangladeshi cooking — not one recipe, but many, each shaped by mustard, oil, spice and timing. Shown here is shorshe ilish, a thick mustard gravy and one of its most recognised forms.

A drier fry and a much lighter jhol follow next — two more entries in the same family, each telling a different part of the same fish's story.

Ilish

Ilish Vaja

Fried, with onion and green chili.

A drier preparation than the gravy versions of ilish — fried until the edges darken, then finished with onion and green chili rather than a sauce.

Ilish

Halka Jhol

The thinnest end of the same spectrum.

Where shorshe ilish is thick and pungent, this jhol stays thin and mild — green chili is the only real seasoning beyond turmeric.

Baila Jhuri

Shredded, not curried.

Jhuri means shredded — the fish is broken down and stir-fried dry with onion and green chili, closer to a bhuna than a curry.

Still being explored

Chingri is a universe of its own.

Malai curry is only one entry point. Bangladeshi prawn cooking carries its own variation in texture, preparation and regional preference — a separate universe from fish, with its own grammar.

Bagda

Bagda

Bagda (tiger prawn) tends toward richer, more substantial preparations in what we've observed so far — built to carry weight in the gravy.

Galda

Galda

Galda (freshwater prawn) often moves toward a different texture and pairing altogether — another branch of the same universe, still being mapped.

Family

Where this story begins

This interest is deeply personal.

My father was connected to the restaurant world around Golanda and Daulatdia during the Pakistan period. My mother became an exceptional home cook, particularly with fish.

Much of what I know came from watching, tasting and remembering. This archive begins there.
